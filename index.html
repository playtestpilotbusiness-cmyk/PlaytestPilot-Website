The problem

You know how your game works. New players do not.

After months of development, it’s hard to see your game the way a new player does. You know where to go, what everything does and how it all fits together, because you built it.

New players arrive with none of that. They walk past prompts, miss objectives and give up before the best part, and most of them never tell you why.

Lost after the tutorial

They finish the tutorial with no idea where to go next.

Blind to the objective

The goal is right there on screen, and they still don’t see it.

Walking past what matters

Nothing told them the lever, the bench or the side path was usable, so they walked on.

Getting it too late

The core mechanic finally clicks, after they’ve stopped having fun.

Questions we answer

What do you need to know about your game?

Tell us, and we’ll build the session around it. Any playable PC build works, at any stage of development. Here are six questions developers often ask.

Ten players start your game with no help or guidance, and we track where they get confused, stuck or lost. Afterwards, the survey asks them to explain, in their own words, what they thought they were supposed to be doing.

  • Tutorial prompts players never noticed
  • Objectives, hints and quest markers players missed or misunderstood
  • Where they hesitate, backtrack or wander off course
  • How long the core mechanic takes to click, and how many players it never clicks for

In the sample report, 7 of 10 players walked straight past the enchantment shrine while its prompt was on screen.

We watch how each player fights and listen to any commentary they give while playing. Afterwards, they rate the combat and explain what felt good and what frustrated them.

  • Whether attacks feel satisfying
  • Whether players can read enemy attacks and the damage they take
  • Which deaths players understand, and which they blame on the game
  • Difficulty spikes that feel unfair

In the sample report, 6 of 10 players couldn’t tell when their attacks connected. One stopped mid-fight to ask, “is this hitting?”

We track every moment a player struggles with your interface. That might be misreading an item’s stats, missing a low-health warning or hunting through the inventory for something they can’t find. Each moment is timestamped, so you can see exactly which screen caused it.

  • Icons and stats players misread
  • HUD warnings and cooldowns they don’t notice
  • Inventory, crafting and skill screens they struggle to navigate
  • Simple actions, like equipping an item, that take far too long

In the sample report, 8 of 10 players got stuck on their first item pickup, taking 21 seconds on average to work out how to equip it.

We watch how players respond to your core loop over the full hour, and compare how they react early on with how they react near the end.

  • Where the variety runs out and play starts to feel repetitive
  • Whether upgrades and rewards keep players engaged
  • Which parts of the loop players look forward to, and which they rush through
  • Whether they wanted to keep playing when the hour was up

In the sample report, combat was still the best-rated part of the game after a full hour, at 4.1 out of 5.

The report tracks what works and what doesn’t. We look at what excites players during their session and what they praise and rate highest in the survey. That tells you what to protect as you make changes, and what to show off in your trailer and on your Steam page.

In the sample report, the boss fight was one of three things that clearly worked, alongside combat feel and atmosphere. One player wrote, “The boss fight was genuinely exciting. I wasn’t expecting that from a demo.”

Emberwake Hollow v0.3.2 has a strong core. Combat was the best-rated part of the survey (4.1 out of 5), atmosphere and audio were praised without prompting, and all 6 testers who reached the Boss Zone beat it within the hour.

The problem is getting there. Enchanting, the system the game’s progression is built on, was walked past by 7 of 10 players in the tutorial, and the first equip stalled 8 of 10 for an average of 21 seconds. Every tester who failed to reach the boss had skipped enchanting, and 4 of 10 say they would have stopped before the hour if they hadn’t been paid to play.

Positive signal: Combat feel and atmosphere carry the session. “Atmospheric” was the most common single word in open answers, and 5 of the 6 testers who beat the boss reacted positively out loud.

Watch: Level 3 lost 4 players. All four backtracked looking for something they thought they had missed, and all four had skipped enchanting. Part of this may be knock-on from the tutorial, so re-test it after the tutorial changes (see 06).

Highest priority: 7 of 10 players walked past the enchantment shrine with the prompt on screen. Those who skipped it described Level 1 as hard rather than recognising they were under-powered.

Your questions, answered

Q1

Do players understand what to do without help?

Not in the first 20 minutes. 7 of 10 walked past the enchantment shrine in the tutorial, 8 of 10 got stuck on their first equip, and “I knew what I was supposed to be doing” averaged 2.3 out of 5. Most had found their feet by Level 2.

Q2

Does combat feel good to play?

Yes. Combat was the best-rated part of the survey at 4.1 out of 5, and 7 of 10 said something positive about it during their first fight. The one weakness is hit feedback: 6 of 10 couldn’t always tell when their attacks connected, especially against fast enemies.

06Recommendations

Focus areas for the next build

Ordered by player impact. Each one names the problem and the direction. How to solve it, and what it costs to build, is your team’s call: you know the trade-offs, we know what players did.

P1
HighTutorial · Addresses Finding #1

Make enchanting something new players do, not something they pass

The tutorial offers the enchantment shrine but doesn’t make it matter. A visible prompt alone did not stop 7 of 10 players walking past, and every tester who skipped it lost time later. Direction: players should leave the tutorial having enchanted something and knowing why it made them stronger.

P2
HighUI / UX · Addresses Finding #2

Make the first equip obvious

8 of 10 players hesitated at their first item pickup, for 21 seconds on average. Direction: at that first pickup, players need to see where the item goes and how to put it there, without searching the menus to find out.

P3
MediumLevel design · Addresses Finding #3

Give players a reason to push on through Level 3

4 players backtracked here looking for something they thought they had missed. All four had also skipped enchanting, so re-test this after P1 before redesigning it. Direction: on entering Level 3, players need to know they are on the right path and that something lies ahead.

P4
MediumCombat feel · Addresses Finding #4

Make it clear when an attack hits

6 of 10 players couldn’t tell whether hits were connecting, most of all against faster enemies where animation and movement overlap. Direction: the moment of impact needs to read clearly at any enemy speed. A re-test should check whether the “is this hitting?” comments go away.

Carry the atmosphere through the boss fight

4 of the 6 who reached the boss found the mix jarring, after praising the audio up to that point. Direction: keep the atmosphere audible under the boss fight’s effects, so a strength doesn’t turn into a complaint at the climax.
